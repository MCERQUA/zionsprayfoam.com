# Blog: Open Cell vs. Closed Cell Spray Foam — Which Is Right for Your Southern Utah Home?

**Date:** 2026-09-24
**Meta title:** Open Cell vs. Closed Cell Spray Foam Insulation Explained
**Meta description:** The real differences between open-cell and closed-cell spray foam insulation, and which one actually fits your application in a southern Utah climate.

## Two Products, Not One "Spray Foam"

"Spray foam insulation" isn't a single product — open-cell and closed-cell foam are genuinely different materials with different densities, different performance characteristics, and different ideal applications. Picking the wrong one for your situation doesn't just mean paying more or less than necessary; it can mean the insulation doesn't perform the way you're expecting for your specific space.

## What Makes Them Different at a Material Level

Closed-cell spray foam has a denser cell structure, where the tiny cells that make up the foam are fully closed off from each other. That density is what gives closed-cell foam its higher R-value per inch, its rigidity, and its resistance to moisture — air and water have a much harder time passing through a closed-cell structure. Open-cell foam has cells that remain open and interconnected, which makes it lighter, more flexible, and less expensive to produce and install, but with a lower R-value per inch and less inherent moisture resistance.

## R-Value: Why It's Not the Whole Story

Closed-cell foam typically delivers something in the range of 6-7 R-value per inch, while open-cell foam typically delivers around 3.5-4 R-value per inch — roughly half. That sounds like closed-cell is simply "better," but R-value per inch isn't the only variable that matters. Open-cell foam expands more per unit of material, filling larger cavities more economically, and its air-sealing performance (which matters as much as raw R-value in a climate like southern Utah's) is strong even at a lower R-value per inch. The right choice depends on the application, the available cavity depth, and the budget — not just which number is higher.

## Moisture Resistance: Where Closed-Cell Pulls Ahead

Closed-cell foam's dense structure makes it significantly more moisture-resistant than open-cell foam, which is why it's the standard choice for applications where moisture is a real concern — crawl spaces with ground moisture exposure, metal buildings prone to condensation, and roofline applications exposed to potential water intrusion at seams and penetrations. Open-cell foam, by contrast, is vapor-permeable, which can actually be an advantage in certain wall assemblies where some vapor movement is desirable, but a liability in applications where moisture control is the primary concern.

## Cost and Structural Considerations

Closed-cell foam costs more per square foot than open-cell, largely because it requires more material by weight to achieve the same coverage and its production process is more involved. It also adds meaningful structural rigidity to whatever it's applied to — a real secondary benefit in applications like metal building roofs or older roof decks that can use the added strength. Open-cell foam doesn't add the same structural contribution, but its lower cost makes it the practical choice for large-volume applications like standard attic floors where structural rigidity isn't the goal.

## Sound Dampening: An Underrated Open-Cell Advantage

Open-cell foam's more flexible, less dense structure actually makes it a better sound dampener than closed-cell foam in many applications — a detail that matters for interior wall applications in new construction, or metal buildings where noise from rain and wind is a real comfort issue. If sound control matters as much as thermal performance for your project, that's worth discussing specifically.

## Vapor Permeability and Wall Assembly Design

Beyond attics and crawl spaces, foam type matters for interior and exterior wall assemblies too. Open-cell foam is vapor-permeable, meaning moisture vapor can pass through it — which sounds like a downside but is actually the correct behavior for certain wall assembly designs that rely on vapor being able to dry toward one side. Closed-cell foam acts as its own vapor barrier, which is the right call in assemblies designed around stopping vapor movement entirely, like many below-grade or exterior-sheathing applications. Getting this wrong — using a vapor-impermeable product in an assembly designed to dry, or vice versa — can create moisture problems inside a wall that aren't visible until they've caused real damage, which is why wall-assembly foam decisions should account for the whole system, not just the insulation layer in isolation.

## Weight and Structural Load Considerations

Closed-cell foam is significantly heavier than open-cell foam per unit of coverage, which occasionally matters for structural applications — a roof deck being converted to a conditioned assembly, for example, needs to be able to support the added weight of a thick closed-cell application, something worth confirming for older structures rather than assuming it's a non-issue. Open-cell foam's lighter weight makes it the more forgiving choice when structural load is a genuine concern.

## How We Decide Which One Fits Your Project

We match foam type to application, not the other way around. Attics typically call for open-cell unless there's a specific reason for closed-cell (like very limited cavity depth). Crawl spaces, roofline applications, and metal buildings typically call for closed-cell because of the added moisture resistance and rigidity. New construction sometimes uses a hybrid approach — closed-cell where moisture resistance matters most, open-cell elsewhere. ## The Bottom Line

Neither product is universally "better" — they're built for different jobs. Closed-cell wins on R-value per inch, moisture resistance, and structural contribution; open-cell wins on cost, coverage efficiency, and sound dampening. The right call depends on what your specific space actually needs.
